Compensation study methodology: from job matching to recommendations

By NovaPath · Published September 11, 2026

A useful compensation study makes the path from evidence to recommendation visible. Start with the decision, define comparable work, choose relevant sources, and explain the limits before interpreting differences in pay.

1. Define the decision and boundaries

Write down the roles, locations, pay elements, and decision the report should support. A review of starting salaries differs from a review of an entire salary structure. Record whether the analysis covers base pay, employer-paid benefits, or both. Agree on the deliverables and the intended reviewers before collecting data.

2. Match the work, not only the title

Build a role profile using duties, required credentials, experience expectations, supervisory responsibilities, and work schedule. Compare those characteristics with the benchmark description. Keep a short explanation for each match and note partial matches rather than forcing a neat answer.

For example, two positions called “teacher” may have different age groups, credentials, supervisory duties, and annual schedules. A title match alone does not establish comparable work.

3. Select and document market evidence

State why each geography and comparison group is relevant. Record source dates, definitions, and coverage. Distinguish published ranges from actual pay observations, and distinguish employer-specific information from aggregated estimates.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes occupational wage estimates for national, state, metropolitan, and nonmetropolitan areas. These provide context; the occupation definition still needs to fit the role being analyzed. See the BLS OEWS methodology and FAQ.

4. Normalize pay and isolate benefits

Put comparable pay on the same basis using documented work schedules and pay definitions. Keep base pay separate from benefits unless the analysis explicitly constructs a total-compensation measure. Do not substitute a generic full-time schedule when the actual contracted schedule is different.

5. Explain findings and uncertainty

Show the selected measure, the organization’s comparable figure, the difference, and the limitation beside each result. Identify weak matches or missing information. A market reference informs a decision; it does not automatically determine the right salary or establish a compliance conclusion.

6. Separate recommendations from decisions

Explain the options and assumptions behind recommendations. Leadership then weighs budget, role design, internal consistency, and implementation needs. Keep draft findings separate from approved changes, and consolidate reviewer feedback before finalizing.
